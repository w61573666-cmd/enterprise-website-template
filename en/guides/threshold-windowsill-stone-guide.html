Threshold & Window Sill Stone: Damming, Drip Edge and Measuring

Home › Stone Guides › Threshold & Window Sill Stone: Damming, Drip Edge and Measuring

Threshold and window sill stones are small pieces, but they directly decide whether your home leaks, grows mould, or drips on the neighbours below. Many owners focus on the big floor and wall finishes, only to discover a wet doorway or a bubbling window frame after moving in. This guide covers the damming role of thresholds, standard bathroom threshold height, window sill drip edges, on-site measuring steps and the common root causes of leaks, so you can get these details right the first time.

Key takeaway

A threshold is functional, not decorative: it must sit above the drainage level, stay stable and be well sealed. A window sill is not just a flat slab — its bottom needs a drip edge (beak) to shed rainwater outward. Get these wrong and the repair cost can exceed the stone itself.

What does a threshold do? The damming role

A threshold stone, also called a transition strip, fundamentally divides zones and stops water. In compact Hong Kong flats, between wet zones like bathrooms, kitchens and dressing rooms and the dry living areas, a threshold slightly separates the two floor heights. When the bathroom floor has a little standing water or mopping creates excess, the threshold acts like a low dike, stopping water from spreading to the living room and protecting wood flooring, skirting and furniture from water damage. Beyond that, the threshold hides the height difference and joint between two materials (say marble on one side, wood on the other), looking neater and lasting longer. Across thousands of residential projects, Hengsheng has seen that a well-made threshold keeps a home dry and odour-free for a decade.

How high should the bathroom threshold be? Standard vs reality

On bathroom threshold height, the common saying "higher blocks more" needs balance. Typically a residential bathroom threshold sits about 15–25mm (roughly half an inch to one inch) above the inner floor. This stops daily mopping and shower splash while not tripping the elderly or children. Too low (5–10mm) and a blocked drain or wide spray flows over; too high (over 40mm) is dangerous for the elderly and wheelchairs and easy to slip on. Another point: the threshold top should slope slightly back into the bathroom, so any water at the door flows inward rather than seeping out. When Hengsheng measures, we also check drainage, floor drain position and the gap under the door frame before deciding the final threshold height — never a single fixed number.

What is a window sill drip edge and why it matters

A window sill drip edge (also called a beak edge or drip line) is a slightly protruding, downward-facing lip on the bottom of the sill. Its function is direct: in rain, water running off the sill surface moves outward; if the underside is flat, water creeps inward along the stone bottom, soaking the window frame, wall and even dripping onto the unit below. The drip edge acts like an eave, breaking the water flow at the protrusion so droplets fall vertically instead of creeping in. Hong Kong's frequent rain and typhoons make this edge essential. When ordering a sill, tell the fabricator to form a drip edge on the underside rather than just a flat slab. For old sills, a drip strip can be added as a fix, but it never beats an integral stone edge for neatness and durability.

How to measure thresholds and sills: on-site steps

These small pieces hate wrong measurements because stone is custom-cut. Hengsheng's on-site measuring usually follows: first, measure the clear opening — actual width and depth of the doorway or window, minus the joint allowance on each side (typically 3–5mm for sealant); second, check the site level with a laser level to determine threshold thickness and height from the floor difference; third, inspect the door frame and floor drain — threshold height is limited by the door frame bottom and drainage, not freely raised; fourth, for windows measure the wall render thickness and window frame protrusion to reserve the drip edge. Best practice is to consider the adjacent floor material thickness (marble, wood, tile) together, confirm a drawing, then place the order. Relying on a rough tape measure to buy off-the-shelf pieces fails nine times out of ten.

Common leak root causes at thresholds and sills

From Hengsheng's repair cases, leaks at thresholds and sills are almost never the stone itself but the joint and slope. The three most common threshold causes: sealant between threshold and floor is too thin or cracked with age, letting water through the gap; the bathroom floor slope is reversed, pooling water toward the door; or the threshold is too low to block water. Sill leaks are mostly missing drip edges or no anti-mould sealant between frame and stone, letting rain seep into the wall. So prevention is not about buying pretty stone but "use anti-mould sealant, correct the slope, form the drip edge". Done well, the stone lasts ten years; done poorly, even expensive marble blackens and moulds.

Which material: marble, granite or engineered stone?

Material choice depends on location and look. Marble (e.g. white or beige marble) has beautiful veining and high class — great as a sill — but as natural calcium carbonate it fears acid and moisture, so a doorway that gets stepped on and knocked is a bit delicate and needs regular sealing. Granite is hard, wear-resistant and low-absorption (usually 0.2–0.5%), making it the most durable and the functional first choice for thresholds. Engineered stone (quartz) is seamless, uniform and crack-resistant, but watch for yellowing in strong sun. For rain-facing east/south sills, granite or sealed marble is advised; thresholds are safest in granite. Budget-wise, granite threshold installed runs about HK$200–$500/ft, marble about HK$300–$800/ft — prices indicative; final quote depends on spec and market.

Installation and edging notes

Installing thresholds and sills, the craftsman's skill varies widely. The threshold must be laid solid with semi-dry cement slurry or stone adhesive — never "spot bedded" with gaps, or it sounds hollow and cracks underfoot; both sides meeting the floor need continuous anti-mould sealant. The sill should slope outward slightly (about 2–3 degrees), with an integral bottom drip edge, and anti-mould sealant between stone and frame. Every Hengsheng slab passes 68 quality checks including thickness, level, edge chipping and drip-edge shape before site delivery, reducing rework. Finally, measure and order the threshold only after tiling and waterproofing are done and the floor is set — ordering too early risks mismatched dimensions and redo.

Whenever your home involves thresholds and sills — these detail joints — it is worth asking a team like Hengsheng to measure on-site. These spots test skill and experience: level, slope, door-frame clearance and drain position must be considered together, and DIY measuring easily errs. Frequently Asked Questions

Should the threshold be higher or lower?

Not the higher the better. A residential bathroom threshold usually sits about 15–25mm above the inner floor — enough to block daily water yet not trip anyone; over 40mm is dangerous for the elderly and wheelchairs. The final height depends on door-frame bottom, drain level and actual site level, decided by the measurer.

Why does a window sill need a drip edge?

The drip edge (beak) is a downward lip on the underside; in rain it breaks the water flow so droplets fall vertically instead of creeping inward to soak the wall or drip below. With Hong Kong's rain and typhoons, this edge is essential — specify it when ordering.

How much do threshold and sill stones cost?

Depends on material and size. Granite threshold installed is about HK$200–$500/ft, marble about HK$300–$800/ft — prices indicative, final quote depends on spec and market. Sills are priced by length and also vary with stone variety.

Why does the doorway always leak?

Nine times out of ten it is not the stone but the joint: sealant too thin or cracked with age, reversed floor slope, or a threshold too low to block water. Good anti-mould sealant and slope matter more than pretty stone.

Granite or marble for the threshold?

Doorways get stepped on and knocked, so granite (hard, wear-resistant, low absorption) is most durable and safest; marble looks beautiful but fears acid and moisture, better as a sill accent and needs regular sealing as a threshold.

Can I measure myself and buy off-the-shelf pieces?

Not advised. Stone is custom-cut; doorways and windows need joint allowance deducted, plus level, frame and drain checks — rough DIY measuring fails nine times out of ten. Free on-site measuring with a confirmed drawing saves far more than a wrong buy.
