Marble Facade Dry-hanging Guide: Systems, Thickness, Back-net and Acceptance

Home › Stone Guides › Marble Facade Dry-hanging Guide: Systems, Thickness, Back-net and Acceptance

A marble facade is the classic finish for premium offices, hotels and luxury homes — opulent and full of presence. But marble (limestone-family) is naturally soft (Mohs ~3, higher absorption than granite); hanging it dozens of floors up without falling, cracking or warping relies entirely on the "dry-hanging system" and strict workmanship. This guide explains dry-hang system types, slab thickness, back-net reinforcement, and the three acceptance essentials: hollow, flatness and joint width.

Key takeaway

Never wet-bed marble onto a wall — alkaline mortar water seeps into the marble and yellows it over time. The correct method is dry-hanging: stainless brackets fix the marble to a steel frame with an air gap behind. Which bracket, how thick the slab, whether there is a back-net, and whether the inspector taps for hollows decides if that facade lasts ten years or two.

Why marble facades must be dry-hung

Marble is calcium carbonate with absorption about 0.3–1% by variety and micro-pores on the surface. Wet-bedding with cement mortar lets alkaline water slowly seep in, yellowing, efflorescing and even powdering the marble; plus wet beds load unevenly and shed under high-rise wind vibration. Dry-hanging solves all this: metal brackets hold the slab back, not mortar grip; an 80–120mm air gap behind ventilates, resists damp and allows thermal movement, and makes individual replacement easy. So every proper Hong Kong marble facade is dry-hung.

Three dry-hang systems: pin, clip, undercut bolt

There are three common systems. First, pin anchor: a shallow side hole holds a stainless pin onto the frame — simple and cheap, but small load point, suited to low buildings or indoor feature walls, not ideal for high-rise. Second, channel / clip (butterfly): a groove at the slab edge is clipped by stainless, load more even than pins — the common Hong Kong commercial choice. Third, undercut bolt: a flared reverse-cone hole at the slab back locks a bolt at the centre, no edge damage, best seismic resistance and safest for high-rise and large slabs, but pricier in labour and parts. All use 304 or 316 stainless to avoid rust staining.

Slab thickness: how thick is safe

For external dry-hung marble, slab thickness should not go below 25mm, commonly 25–30mm; for large formats (e.g. over 1200×600mm) or high-rise windward positions, step up to 30–35mm or more to reduce deflection from wind load and self-weight. Thickness must be even — uneven slabs hang crooked. Back-net: insurance for marble facades

Marble is soft and most prone to edge chips and hairline cracks in transport and installation. The back-net sticks a layer of fibreglass mesh on the slab back and floods resin into the stone pores, so the whole slab acts as one piece — far better impact and crack resistance. Quality works use a double back-net or stainless fine mesh for extra strength. Crucially, the back-net resin must be weather- and moisture-resistant; never use cheap hydrolysable glue or the net detaches in years and the marble loosens. Waterproofing, damp proofing and joint sealing

Dry-hanging leaves an air gap, but joints remain the entry for water and wind. Marble facades use weather- and mould-resistant sealant (modified silicone or polyurethane), not ordinary glass glue that ages black and runs. Before sealing, the slab edge gets a waterproof primer so rain cannot creep into the air gap. Hong Kong's typhoons and rain demand drainage weeps and flashing so any ingress drains away, never pools. Back-net, brackets and gaskets all must resist damp and rust — these hidden parts decide facade life.

Three acceptance checks: hollow, flatness, joint width

On completion, check these three. First, hollow: tap the slab with a hard tool (or hollow hammer); a solid "tok" is fine, a hollow "clack" means the slab is not seated on the bracket and loads badly — must be re-done. Dry-hang theoretically hollows less than wet-bed, but loose brackets or detached back-net still sound hollow. Second, flatness: a 2-metre straightedge against the face — step difference usually ≤ 2mm (≤ 1mm for premium); otherwise raking light shows waves. Use string lines and a level across the whole wall. Third, joint width: joints even, commonly 6–10mm, deviation ≤ 1mm; uneven gaps make the whole facade look shoddy. Owner and installer should tap and measure together on site — don't sign off then discover.

Hong Kong weather and high-rise notes

Hong Kong has many typhoons, strong UV and acid rain, so marble facades need extra care. Windward and upper floors use seismic-strong systems like undercut bolts with frames sized for wind pressure; marble gets a protective sealer beforehand to cut absorption and acid etch; dark marble expands in sun, so leave slightly wider joints than indoors. Also cap the top with coping stone and drip lines to stop water running back into joints. These are Hong Kong installers' field lessons — ask before ordering.

Maintenance and renovation

Do not treat a marble facade like glass and blast it with a pressure washer — high pressure forces water into joints. Every 1–2 years, hire pros with neutral cleaner and soft brush, checking sealant ageing and bracket looseness. If the surface dulls, arrange cradle access for crystallisation or re-sealing. Replace any single cracked or discoloured slab early to stop spread. Well maintained, a marble facade stays beautiful for over a decade.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which dry-hang system for a marble facade?

Low buildings or indoor feature walls can use pin anchor; Hong Kong offices commonly use clip (butterfly) for even load; high-rise and large slabs prefer undercut bolt for best seismic safety. All use 304/316 stainless, 316 near coast.

How thick must facade marble slabs be?

External dry-hang should not be below 25mm, commonly 25–30mm; large formats or high-rise windward positions step to 30–35mm+ to cut wind-load deflection. Thickness must be even; calculate by height and wind before ordering.

Is back-net reinforcement necessary?

Yes. Marble is soft and chips or hairline-cracks in transport; the back-net (fibreglass mesh + resin) makes the slab one piece, crack- and impact-resistant. Premium works use double back-net with weather-resistant resin, never cheap hydrolysable glue.

How to check hollow on acceptance?

Tap each slab with a hollow hammer; a solid 'tok' is normal, a hollow 'clack' means the slab is not seated on the bracket and loads badly — re-do it. Tap every slab, none missed.

What are the flatness and joint-width standards?

A 2-metre straightedge: step difference usually ≤ 2mm (≤ 1mm premium); joints 6–10mm, deviation ≤ 1mm, even. Both show ugly under raking sunlight, so measure with string lines and level across the wall.

What does a marble facade cost per sq ft?

Marble material about HK$150–$600 per sq ft by origin and variety; dry-hang labour, frame and back-net add about HK$300–$800/ft. Prices are indicative; final quote depends on spec and market.
